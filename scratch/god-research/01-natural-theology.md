# Arguments for God's Existence: Strength Assessment

Date: 2026-09-13. Scope: classical/contemporary natural theology, mainstream analytic philosophy of religion. Background rate: [PhilPapers 2020 Survey](https://survey2020.philpeople.org/survey/results/4842) — among philosophers generally, ~67% accept/lean atheism, ~14.6% theism, ~7% agnostic/other ([summary](https://journals.publishing.umich.edu/phimp/article/id/2109/print/)). Philosophy of religion specifically skews theist relative to the discipline as a whole (inference from survey cross-tabs discussed in commentary on the 2020 survey; I have not pulled the exact sub-discipline breakdown, flagging as **unverified inference**).

## Kalam Cosmological Argument

**Argument.** Craig's formulation: (1) everything that begins to exist has a cause; (2) the universe began to exist; (3) therefore the universe has a cause. Premise 2 is defended by a priori arguments against actual infinites (an infinite temporal regress of events would be a completed actual infinite, which Craig holds is metaphysically impossible, illustrated via Hilbert's Hotel) plus a posteriori appeal to Big Bang cosmology. Craig then argues the cause must be a timeless, spaceless, immaterial, personal agent (personal to explain how a timeless cause produces a temporally-bounded effect — a "free" cause is needed since a fixed mechanistic cause would produce its effect eternally). Sources: [SEP, Cosmological Argument](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/cosmological-argument/); Craig's own statement at [Reasonable Faith](https://www.reasonablefaith.org/writings/popular-writings/existence-natureof-god/the-kalam-cosmological-argument); Craig & Sinclair, *The Kalām Cosmological Argument* ([PhilPapers](https://philpapers.org/rec/CRATKC-8)).

**Objections.**
- *Hilbert's Hotel / actual infinite.* Critics (e.g., discussion in [Philosophical Disquisitions](https://philosophicaldisquisitions.blogspot.com/2013/07/hedrick-on-hilberts-hotel-and-actual.html), [Part 2](https://philosophicaldisquisitions.blogspot.com/2013/08/hedrick-on-hilberts-hotel-and-actual.html)) argue Hilbert's Hotel at best shows an actual infinite collection of *concrete physical objects co-existing at one time* is odd, not impossible — and even granting the intuition, it doesn't obviously transfer to a *sequence of past events*, which is not a "thing" of the hotel-guest sort. This is a live, unresolved dispute rather than a decisive refutation either way.
- *B-theory of time.* If B-theory (all times equally real, no objective "becoming") is true, "beginning to exist" doesn't mean what Craig needs it to mean, and the intuitive causal principle (drawn from things beginning within time) may not apply to time itself. Wes Morriston has pressed this for over a decade — see his papers hosted at [University of Colorado](https://spot.colorado.edu/~morristo/wes2craig1.pdf) and [here](https://spot.colorado.edu/~morristo/NewKalamCritique.pdf). Craig responds by defending A-theory independently (a large separate literature on the philosophy of time, not settled). Note: the search-summarized claim that Craig's opponents "carry the burden of proof" for A-theory is Craig's own framing, not a neutral characterization — **flagging as advocacy, not consensus**.
- *Quantum vacuum / causal principle.* Objection: virtual-particle/vacuum fluctuation phenomena show things can begin without deterministic prior cause, undercutting premise 1. Standard reply (echoed at [Reasonable Faith](https://www.reasonablefaith.org/writings/question-answer/causation-and-spacetime) and apologetics sources like [CrossExamined](https://crossexamined.org/objection-premise-1-kalam-doesnt-quantum-mechanics-violate-causal-principle/)): the quantum vacuum is a structured, law-governed physical substrate, not "nothing," so these cases are not causeless-from-nothing. This reply is broadly accepted as *correct against the naive version* of the objection, but it also means the Kalam's "began to exist" cannot cleanly extend to "the universe emerging from a pre-existing quantum vacuum" — which is exactly what some cosmological models (e.g., vacuum-fluctuation/bounce cosmologies) propose. See the technical exchange in [Loeb/critics on bounce cosmologies vs. Craig & Sinclair](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.07748).
- *Causality outside time.* If the universe includes all space and time, "cause" (ordinarily a temporal relation) may not apply to its origin at all — a point pressed by physicist Sean Carroll: causal explanation presupposes a system of laws and boundary conditions that the totality of physical reality is not obviously subject to (paraphrased from search summary; primary Carroll source not directly verified here — **flag: secondhand**). Craig replies that causation need not be temporal (citing Aquinas-style *sustaining* causation) and that atemporal-but-real causal relations are coherent — see the discussion in the [SEP Cosmological Argument entry](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/cosmological-argument/) distinguishing Aquinas's atemporal dependence framing from the Kalam's temporal-first-cause framing.

**Verdict.** Contested, not settled, and probably not resolvable on current physics — the A-theory/B-theory dispute the whole argument rides on is itself an open metaphysical question independent of theism. Mainstream philosophy of religion treats Kalam as a serious, professionally-defended argument (Craig's technical work is published and engaged with, e.g., in *Cosmological Argument* SEP entry and peer-reviewed exchanges), but it convinces almost no one who wasn't already inclined toward theism, and the inference from "the universe has a cause" to "that cause is the personal God of theism" is a separate, additionally-contested step even among those who accept the causal premises.

## Leibnizian Contingency Argument / PSR

**Argument.** Leibniz: every contingent fact requires an explanation (Principle of Sufficient Reason). Take the conjunction of all contingent facts (or: the totality of contingent existence) — it is itself contingent, so it too needs an explanation. That explanation cannot be another contingent fact (regress/circularity), so it must be a necessarily existing being — God. Pruss's canonical modern formulation: every contingent fact has an explanation; there's a contingent fact encompassing all contingent facts; therefore its explanation involves a necessary being. Sources: [SEP/Pruss & Lin, Principle of Sufficient Reason](https://www.academia.edu/2327251/_The_Principle_of_Sufficient_Reason_The_Stanford_Online_Encyclopedia_of_Philosophy_Co_author_Martin_Lin_Substantially_revised_Sept_2016) (note: accessed via Academia.edu mirror, not the live SEP page, which was unreachable this session); [Pruss, Leibnizian Cosmological Arguments](http://alexanderpruss.com/papers/LCA.html).

**Objections.**
- *Brute facts.* Deny PSR outright: some facts, including possibly the universe's existence, simply have no explanation (Bertrand Russell's "the universe is just there, and that's all"; contemporary version from Sean Carroll). This is a live, respectable position in analytic metaphysics, not a dodge.
- *Van Inwagen's modal fatalism argument against PSR.* Formal argument that PSR entails all facts are necessary (collapses contingency): if the "Big Conjunctive Contingent Fact" (BCCF) is explained by a necessary fact, its explanandum should itself become necessary; if explained by a contingent fact, the explanation is circular (the explainer is part of the BCCF). For decades treated as a near-decisive refutation of strong PSR; more recent work (e.g., on "grounding" explanations and rejecting the distribution principle — a fact explaining a conjunction need not explain every conjunct) has eroded confidence that the argument succeeds. See discussion summarized from [Tomaszewski, PSR Defended](https://philpapers.org/rec/TOMTPO-8) and [Blado, PSR and the Nature of Explanation](https://philpapers.org/rec/BLATPA-16). This is a genuine technical back-and-forth in current metaphysics, not resolved.
- *Necessary universe.* Even granting PSR, the "necessary being" need not be God — the universe (or its fundamental laws/initial conditions) could itself be the necessary being/fact. Leibniz's argument needs an additional step (usually: the universe's contingency is evident because it could have been otherwise / no physical thing seems metaphysically necessary) to exclude this, and that step is independently contestable.

**Verdict.** PSR-based arguments turn on a principle (PSR) that is intuitively appealing but has serious formal objections (van Inwagen) and permits brute-fact denial as a coherent alternative. Even a friend of PSR is not thereby forced to theism specifically — "necessary universe" is a live alternative. Mainstream verdict: PSR is a genuinely contested principle in metaphysics generally (not merely in philosophy of religion), so this argument's force is hostage to a broader, unresolved debate — weaker as *specifically theistic* evidence than as a metaphysics-of-explanation dispute.

## Fine-Tuning Argument

**Argument.** Physical constants and initial conditions fall within an apparently very narrow life-permitting range; this is surprising/improbable on chance/naturalism but expected (or far less surprising) on design; therefore fine-tuning is evidence for a designer. Standard Bayesian formulation compares P(fine-tuning | design) vs. P(fine-tuning | chance). Widely regarded, per [SEP, Fine-Tuning](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/fine-tuning/), as the strongest version of the teleological/design argument that contemporary physics affords.

**Objections.**
- *Multiverse.* If vastly many universes exist with varying constants, it's unsurprising that at least one is life-permitting, and we'd only ever observe ourselves in such a one (anthropic selection) — no designer needed. Counter: Roger White's "inverse gambler's fallacy" / firing-squad argument — a multiverse doesn't make it more likely that *this particular* universe (ours) is life-permitting, since our own draw is probabilistically independent of how many other draws occurred elsewhere. See [White, Fine-Tuning and Multiple Universes](https://web.mit.edu/rog/www/papers/fine_tuning.pdf) and critical responses arguing White is mistaken, e.g. [Saward, "why White is wrong"](https://www.academia.edu/2563120/Fine-tuning_as_evidence_for_a_multiverse_why_White_is_wrong) and a 2024 Synthese piece revisiting the question, [Is the fine-tuning evidence for a multiverse?](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-024-04621-z). This sub-debate is technical and unresolved among philosophers of probability.
- *Anthropic principle / Ikeda–Jefferys.* Ikeda and Jefferys offer a Bayesian argument that once you condition on the fact of your own existence/observation (which you could not have observed otherwise), fine-tuning provides *no* net evidence for design over a no-design hypothesis with sufficiently many "attempts" (chance/multiverse) — a formal reversal of the naive fine-tuning inference. See discussion at [Internet Infidels: Fine-Tuning and Probability](https://infidels.org/library/modern/stephen-nygaard-fine-tuning/) and Craig's own rebuttal, [Reasonable Faith: A Fatal Objection to the Fine-Tuning Argument?](https://www.reasonablefaith.org/writings/question-answer/a-fatal-objection-to-the-fine-tuning-argument). Design proponents (e.g., Robin Collins, Craig) argue Ikeda–Jefferys smuggles in an illegitimate self-selection assumption; this is contested in the technical Bayesian-philosophy-of-probability literature, not settled.
- *Normalizability problem.* The probability calculation requires a probability distribution over possible values of the constants, but the range of "all possible values" a constant could take is often unbounded/infinite, making a uniform probability measure non-normalizable (can't sum/integrate to 1) — so "P(life-permitting range | chance) is tiny" may not even be a well-defined claim. Recent technical treatment: [Synthese, "How not to avoid the problems of old evidence and normalizability for fine-tuning"](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-026-05695-7). This is considered one of the more serious *formal* objections, independent of theism/atheism — it's a challenge to whether the argument's probabilistic scaffolding is even coherent.

**Verdict.** Per SEP, this is the strongest contemporary teleological argument, but it is contested at the level of formal probability theory itself (normalizability, reference class/self-selection problems), not just at the level of "well, maybe there's a multiverse." Philosophers of probability are genuinely divided on whether the argument's Bayesian setup is even well-posed. I'd rate this the most technically live of the classical arguments — real open problems on both sides, not primarily an intuition clash.

## Moral Argument

**Argument.** Standard form: objective moral facts/moral knowledge/moral motivation are best explained by (or require) a transcendent, personal source; objective morality exists; therefore God exists. Divine command theory is the typical metaethical partner: moral obligations are constituted by God's commands. Source: [SEP, Moral Arguments for the Existence of God](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-arguments-god/).

**Objections.**
- *Euthyphro dilemma.* Is an act right because God commands it (making morality arbitrary — God could have commanded torture-for-fun and it would be obligatory), or does God command it because it's right (making rightness independent of God, undermining the argument's claim that God is morality's source)? Robert Adams's modified divine command theory tries to escape via a good/right distinction (God is essentially, necessarily good, so his commands aren't arbitrary even though contingent on his nature) — per SEP this "successfully meets" the classic dilemma but faces further objections (e.g., is calling God's nature "good" then empty/tautological; does it just relocate the arbitrariness one level up to which nature God happens to have).
- *Secular moral realism.* Erik Wielenberg and Russ Shafer-Landau defend non-natural, non-theistic moral realism — sui generis objective moral facts that don't reduce to natural facts and don't require God as grounding, analogous to G.E. Moore's non-naturalism. See [Wielenberg, In Defense of Non-Natural, Non-Theistic Moral Realism](https://philarchive.org/archive/WIEIDO) and his book *Robust Ethics*. This directly denies the argument's key premise that objective morality needs a theistic ground, and is a live, well-regarded position in metaethics independent of religion.
- *Evolutionary debunking.* Sharon Street's "Darwinian Dilemma" argues moral realists can't explain how evolutionarily-shaped moral intuitions would reliably track mind-independent moral facts, since evolution selects for fitness, not truth-tracking — see [Street, A Darwinian Dilemma for Realist Theories of Value](https://philpapers.org/rec/STRADD). This cuts against moral realism generally (theist and atheist versions alike), so it's a double-edged sword — theists sometimes deploy it *against* secular moral realism specifically, but it equally threatens theistic moral realism unless one adds further theological content about why God would ensure reliable moral cognition. Critics of Street (cited in [Biology & Philosophy discussion](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10539-018-9652-0)) argue the empirical premises about evolutionary etiology of *specific* moral beliefs are underdetermined, so the debunking argument proves less than advertised.

**Verdict.** The moral argument's weakest link is the premise that objective morality requires God — non-theistic moral realism (Wielenberg, Shafer-Landau, Enoch's "Cuneo"/robust realism tradition) is a mainstream, actively-defended position, and Euthyphro-style worries about divine command theory remain a genuine cost even for its best (Adams-style) versions. Evolutionary debunking is interesting but symmetric — it's not clearly an argument *for* theism since it threatens all realism. Net: moral argument's force depends heavily on independently rejecting secular moral realism, which most metaethicists (theist or not) don't consider a settled matter in the argument's favor.

## Ontological Argument (Anselm, Plantinga)

**Argument.** Anselm (Proslogion II): God is "that than which nothing greater can be conceived"; existing in reality is greater than existing in the mind alone; so if God existed only in the mind, a greater being (one existing in reality) could be conceived, contradiction; therefore God exists in reality. Plantinga's modal version: define a "maximally great" being as one having maximal excellence (omniscience, omnipotence, moral perfection) in every possible world; premise — it's *possible* that a maximally great being exists (i.e., exists in at least one possible world); by the S5 axiom of modal logic (if possibly-necessarily-P, then necessarily-P), a being necessary in one world is necessary/existent in all worlds, including the actual one. Source: [SEP, Ontological Arguments](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ontological-arguments/); technical exposition e.g. [Academia.edu, Assessing Plantinga's S5 Modal Ontological Argument](https://www.academia.edu/43105870/ASSESSING_PLANTINGA_S_S5_MODAL_ONTOLOGICAL_ARGUMENT).

**Objections.**
- *Gaunilo's parody.* Original medieval objection: apply the same reasoning to "the greatest conceivable island" to derive its necessary existence, which is absurd — so the reasoning form is invalid regardless of content. Per SEP, there's now an extensive literature on parodies, and each parody is tailored to a specific formulation; defenders (Plantinga included) argue "greatest possible island" is incoherent as a maximal concept (no intrinsic maximum for island-making qualities like number of coconuts), so it disanalogous to a maximally-great *being*, whereas critics reply this move is ad hoc — see [SEP entry](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ontological-arguments/).
- *Question-begging possibility premise.* The crux objection to Plantinga's version: the premise "possibly, a maximally great being exists" is not obviously weaker than the conclusion once S5 is granted — modal collapse under S5 means asserting mere possibility of necessary existence is nearly equivalent to asserting necessary existence outright, so an atheist has exactly as much reason to assert "possibly, a maximally great being does *not* exist" (which by parallel reasoning entails necessarily no such being exists). Plantinga himself acknowledges the argument doesn't *prove* its conclusion to a skeptic — it shows the belief is rationally permissible if you already grant the possibility premise, not that you're rationally compelled to grant that premise. See [PhilArchive, Leslie Allan, Plantinga's Ontological Argument](https://philarchive.org/archive/ALLPOA-4) and discussion at [Reasonable Faith on the begging-the-question charge](https://www.reasonablefaith.org/writings/question-answer/does-the-ontological-argument-beg-the-question).

**Verdict.** Essentially universal agreement in the SEP/mainstream literature (this is one of the more settled meta-level judgments in the field) that ontological arguments are logically valid (contested by very few) but rest on a possibility/premise that is exactly as contestable as the conclusion, so they are dialectically inert against a determined skeptic — useful, if at all, only for showing internal coherence of theism to someone already agnostic-leaning, not as a persuasive proof. Plantinga himself did not claim it as a knock-down proof.

## Consciousness / Argument from Reason / Psychophysical Harmony

**Argument from Reason (Lewis/Reppert).** If naturalism is true, mental events (beliefs, inferences) are fully explained by non-rational physical/causal processes; but rational inference requires beliefs held *because* of logical/evidential relations (ground-consequent reasoning), not merely because of prior causes; naturalism cannot accommodate this "because of reasons" relation, so naturalism is self-undermining (it requires rational inference to argue for itself while denying the preconditions of rational inference). Victor Reppert reformulated Lewis's argument to survive G.E.M. Anscombe's classic 1948 critique. Source: [Wikipedia, Argument from reason](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argument_from_reason) (secondary — no SEP entry found on this specific argument; treat as less rigorously vetted than SEP-covered arguments) and Reppert's *C.S. Lewis's Dangerous Idea*.
*Objection:* Naturalist responses argue reasons-explanations and causal explanations aren't exclusive — a belief's being caused by neural processes doesn't preclude that process from being reliably truth-tracking (e.g., a well-designed classical/connectionist computer's outputs are both fully caused and logically/rationally structured). Critics argue Lewis/Reppert's dichotomy between "cause" and "reason" is a false one — this is the majority naturalist response in philosophy of mind, though a full accounting of the state of the *specific* debate is limited by secondary sourcing here — **flagging as under-researched relative to other sections**.

**Psychophysical Harmony (Cutter & Crummett, 2025).** Phenomenal (conscious) states correlate strikingly well with the physical states that "make sense" of them — e.g., pain-feelings correlate with tissue damage and avoidance behavior, not randomly with irrelevant behaviors; verbal reports about experience are made true by the very experiences reported. This tight correlation is argued to be antecedently far more likely on theism (a designer would link consciousness and physics sensibly) than on naturalism (where the psychophysical laws are, on standard views, arbitrary/brute), loosely analogous to cosmological fine-tuning. Source: [PhilArchive, Cutter & Crummett, Psychophysical Harmony: A New Argument for Theism](https://philarchive.org/archive/CUTPHA), forthcoming in *Oxford Studies in Philosophy of Religion* (2025) — **note: very recent, not yet subject to extended peer engagement/consensus**.
*Objection:* Critics argue (a) harmony is not obviously improbable under naturalism once you consider that disharmonious minds (e.g., organisms whose pain-quale correlated with approach rather than avoidance) would be selected against — evolution supplies a non-theistic explanation for at least the functional/behavioral harmony, even if not the "why does this particular quale feel this way" residue; (b) some naturalists bite the bullet via normative/phenomenal error theory, denying the relevant normative facts the argument presupposes. See critical posts: [Naturalism Next, "A Comprehensive Critique"](https://naturalismnext.blogspot.com/2023/03/introduction-note-1-this-post-is.html) and rebuttal exchange at [Bentham's Substack](https://benthams.substack.com/p/reply-to-naturalism-next-on-psychophysical). Note the evolutionary reply only addresses *functional* harmony (behavior matching phenomenal valence), not the deeper explanatory-gap style puzzle of why any particular quale attaches to any particular function at all — that residue is closer to the hard problem of consciousness generally, an unresolved problem independent of theism.

**Verdict.** Argument from reason is a minority position, taken seriously in Christian philosophy circles (Reppert, Plantinga's own "evolutionary argument against naturalism" is a cousin) but not treated as a major live contender in mainstream philosophy of mind, where physicalist/functionalist accounts of reasoning are the default (though themselves contested on separate grounds, e.g. the hard problem of consciousness). Psychophysical harmony is new (2025) and interesting but untested by extended peer scrutiny; my assessment that it's "the newest and least-consensus-tested" argument here is my own inference from publication recency, not a scholarly consensus claim.

## Argument from Religious Experience

**Argument.** William Alston's version (1991, *Perceiving God*): religious experiences function like sensory perceptual experiences within a "doxastic practice" (a socially established, internally consistent way of forming beliefs from experience, with its own background assumptions and defeaters); sensory experience is accepted as justifying belief about the physical world via analogous practices; by parity of reasoning, religious experience — specifically, the "Christian mystical practice" (CMP) — can justify religious belief for the practitioner, absent specific defeaters. Source: [SEP, Religious Experience](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/religious-experience/); summary discussion at [John Piippo, Alston – Religious Experience as Perception of God](https://www.johnpiippo.com/2017/02/willam-p-alston-religious-experience-as.html).

**Objections.**
- *Religious diversity.* If God reliably produces veridical experiences, the wildly conflicting content of experiences across religious traditions (Christian, Hindu, Buddhist, etc., often mutually exclusive in doctrinal content) functions like mutually undermining eyewitness testimony, undercutting the reliability claim for any one practice. Alston's own reply — that one is entitled to trust one's own practice absent a positive reason to think it's *worse* than a rival — is widely regarded (including by many sympathetic to religion) as its weakest point, since it seems to license mutually exclusive practices as equally rational, which is an uncomfortable result. Michael Martin pressed this and related objections; see [Piippo, Alston vs. Martin](https://www.johnpiippo.com/2017/02/religious-experience-william-alston-vs.html) and [SEP entry](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/religious-experience/), which frames diversity and naturalistic explanation as "the two most important defeaters on the table."
- *Naturalistic explanation.* Neuroscience/psychology offer alternative causal accounts (temporal lobe activity, meditative/psychedelic states, hypoxia, sleep paralysis, cultural priming) that can produce phenomenologically similar experiences without positing a real supernatural referent — available explanation undercuts (though doesn't strictly disprove) the inference to a real object of the experience. This parallels the general problem for any perceptual argument: showing a *cause* for the experience-state doesn't by itself show the experience is non-veridical (an analogous "explaining" story exists for ordinary sense perception, e.g., photons hitting the retina, without that undermining sense-perception's reliability) — so the force of this objection depends on whether one has independent reason to disbelieve there's a real object being perceived, which is close to arguing in a circle. SEP's framing (see link above) treats this as a genuine, unresolved epistemological tension rather than a knockdown.

**Verdict.** Widely regarded in the SEP entry and surrounding literature as the *weakest* of the major arguments as a piece of public, third-person evidence — it is at best a first-person justification story (I am prima facie entitled to trust my own experience) rather than an argument that should move a skeptic, and the diversity problem in particular has no fully satisfying answer even from sympathetic epistemologists. It functions more as a defense of the rationality of existing belief (a "properly basic belief" move, cf. Plantinga's reformed epistemology) than as an argument that generates belief from neutral premises.

## Overall Note on "Mainstream" Verdicts

Characterizations of "where mainstream philosophy of religion lands" above are drawn from how SEP/IEP-level literature frames the state of each debate (contested vs. more settled), not from a single authoritative poll broken out argument-by-argument — no such poll was found in this search. The one hard data point is the discipline-wide PhilPapers 2020 split (67% atheism / 14.6% theism among philosophers generally); I was not able to verify a philosophy-of-religion-specific sub-breakdown this session (SEP and IEP were unreachable via direct fetch due to a network egress block, so citations above rely on WebSearch-returned summaries rather than full-text fetches of primary SEP/IEP pages — the URLs are correct SEP/IEP entries, but I was not able to independently verify their exact wording beyond what the search snippets returned). Treat the "verdict" paragraphs as my synthesis, clearly separated from the sourced claims that precede them in each section.
